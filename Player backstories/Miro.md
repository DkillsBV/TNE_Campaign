Background

No one in Kancandrith's castle ever claimed him. He appeared as an infant at the gates one autumn night — horned, tailed, unmistakably touched by something the local priests muttered about but never named aloud. The staff didn't dare turn him fully away, half out of superstition and half out of pity, but they didn't take him in either. He grew up in the cracks of castle life: stable straw for a bed, kitchen scraps tossed his way like offerings to ward off bad luck, the occasional cuff or curse when a noble's child needed someone to blame. He learned early that people gave him nothing freely. So he learned to take.

The woods beyond the castle's fields were the only place that never flinched from him. He'd slip out whenever Kancandrith grew too cruel, and somewhere in the hills the land seemed to notice him back — a warmth in the dirt, a stillness in the trees that felt like being seen instead of judged. Long before anyone called him a druid, he understood that the wild had claimed him before any person ever had. It didn't ask what he looked like. It only asked if he'd listen.

By ten years old, hunger had made him bold — bold enough to try robbing the wrong camp. A semi-settled band of orcs, living rough on Kancandrith's fringes the same way he lived rough on its edges, caught him mid-theft with his hands still in their stores. He expected to be killed for it. Instead, they laughed. A scrawny, horned child with the nerve to steal from orcs twice his size — that wasn't an insult to them, it was something to respect. They didn't send him back. They fed him properly for the first time in his life, and then they kept him.

Orcish became the first language anyone ever taught him with patience instead of scorn, and he soaked it up like the woods had taught him to soak up quiet. It wasn't just a tongue — it was proof that someone had decided he was worth teaching anything. The clan became the family the castle never let him have, and he became useful to them exactly because of what made him an outcast everywhere else: small enough to slip through gaps a grown orc couldn't, quick enough to vanish before guards even registered a face, and unassuming enough — in a horrifying way — that human settlements underestimated him right up until their purses were lighter. He steals for the clan now not out of debt, but because it's the one thing he can give back to the only people who ever gave him something first.


The Debt

He'd never stolen from a target for any reason but hunger — not until he crossed paths with a caravan that didn't smell right. Cages dressed up as cargo. People, not goods, chained below deck boards and kept quiet with fear. He didn't plan a rescue; he wasn't that reckless. But he took what he could — coin, papers, anything that looked valuable — the same way he'd take from any mark. It was only after, going through what he'd stolen, that he understood exactly what he'd stumbled into.

By then it was too late to disappear cleanly. The caravan belonged to Vaultern Grask, a man who'd built a fortune on human trafficking and made sure no one who saw his operation lived to describe it twice. Grask didn't send local muscle after a single thief — he hired Ilsevet Korr, the finest tracker in the country, a woman rumored never to have failed a contract. Three days later, in a market town far from Kancandrith, she found him.

Grask didn't want him dead — a body draws questions, and questions are bad for business. He wanted him owned. So the offer came instead of the blade: work off what was taken, and more besides, until Grask decides the debt is paid — however long that takes, whatever it requires. No fixed number of jobs. No contract with an end date. Just an obligation that ends when a slaver says it ends, which might be never.

It's the first time in his life someone has had that kind of hold over him. The castle never wanted him enough to control him. The clan never needed to — loyalty did that on its own. Grask is different: cold, patient, and utterly indifferent to whether his tiefling errand-runner lives a long life or a short one, as long as the debt gets paid first. And the clan doesn't even know it's happening. To them, he simply grew up and moved on, the way young ones sometimes do — no goodbye, no explanation, because if he'd told them the truth, they'd have gone to war with a man who could crush them without noticing he'd done it.
