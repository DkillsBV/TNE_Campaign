_The ore-rich highlands north of the Kingdom of Taginumn_

### The Miners' Republic

Grael was never conquered, married into an empire, or born from tragedy — it was built, quite deliberately, on money. Generations ago, dwarven prospectors discovered a rare, ice-touched ore running deep through the highland rock, prized by smiths and engineers across the continent for weapons and tools that could withstand the harshest cold without cracking. What began as a handful of scattered dwarven mining camps — too small individually for any neighboring power to bother annexing — grew wealthy fast enough that by the time larger kingdoms took real notice, the camps had already pooled their fortunes, hired their own guards, and bought enough influence to make annexation more expensive than simply recognizing them as a nation in their own right.

**Dwarves** remain Grael's founding and dominant people — the miners, engineers, and senators whose expertise in stonework and ore-craft built the Republic's fortune in the first place, and whose culture still shapes its architecture, its work ethic, and its blunt, ledger-minded approach to governance. But Grael's mines needed hands faster than dwarven families alone could provide, and the wages were honest enough that word spread. **Humans, gnomes, goliaths, halflings, orcs, elves, and tieflings** all arrived over the generations that followed, drawn by steady work and a nation that, unusually, never asked what you were before it asked what you could do.

### The Twin Seats

That founding pragmatism eventually became formal law. Every race with an established population in Grael holds exactly two seats in the ruling senate — one male, one female — regardless of that race's total numbers or how recently they arrived. A dwarf senator carries no more formal weight than a halfling one, even though dwarves outnumber halflings many times over in the mines below. The arrangement, known as the **Twin Seats**, was written into Grael's charter specifically to prevent its founding race from ever legislating the rest into second-class status simply by weight of numbers — a dwarf-built safeguard against a dwarf-dominated future.

It makes for a senate that looks nothing like Grael's streets or its mine-shafts, and everything like a nation that decided fairness was worth more than proportional convenience. Debates are, by all accounts, exactly as blunt and occasionally chaotic as a chamber built that way would suggest — but Grael has never had a race riot, a secession movement, or a disenfranchised population, which its senators consider proof enough that the system works.

### The Southern Gift

Grael's wealth has always outpaced its willingness to spend it on ambition. When survey teams confirmed that a stretch of Grael's own southern territory — icebound, thin-soiled, and useless for mining or farming — sat far more naturally within reach of Taginumn's frontier settlements than Grael's own mining towns, the senate did something almost unheard of between neighboring nations: it simply gave the land away, no strings attached beyond a standing trade agreement both sides were already happy with. Grael's senators reasoned, with characteristic bluntness, that land they couldn't use was worth infinitely less than a permanently favorable relationship with the second-strongest military on the continent.

Taginumn accepted, more than a little bemused by a gift with no catch attached — a rarity in a world where every other border shift on the continent has come from marriage, conquest, or collapse. The gesture bought Grael something no amount of ore ever could: genuine, unforced goodwill with its most powerful neighbor, one Taginumn has quietly remembered ever since.

### A Republic, Not a Fortress

Grael keeps a modest, well-equipped militia rather than a standing army, funded generously but never expanded beyond what its own defense requires — expansion has simply never been profitable, and the senate has always preferred spending on infrastructure and trade over conquest. Its true defense is the same as it's always been: being far more valuable as a trading partner and ally than as a conquest, and having, in Taginumn, a neighbor with every reason to make sure no one ever tests that theory.
