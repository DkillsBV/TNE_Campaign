_The small, ambitious highland nation beside Firk_

### A Nation Out of Impatience

Virk is barely ten years old, and it knows it — every civic building, every newly minted coin, every over-eager trade agreement carries the slightly try-hard energy of a nation that split off from its parent specifically because it thought it could do things faster and bolder. Where Firk is patient, Virk is restless. Where Firk waits to be needed, Virk goes looking for relevance, aggressively courting trade deals, positioning itself as a mediator between Wesch and Lansoomeshiz, and generally behaving like a nation twice its size and age would.

Virk was born from **the Schism** — a succession dispute within Firk's royal court a decade ago, when a restless younger branch of the royal family wanted Firk to finally act like the regional power its wealth suggested it could be, rather than continue its old, cautious tradition. Rather than fight a civil war over it, Firk's crown let the dissenting faction leave peacefully, granting their contested border territory full independence rather than risk tearing the kingdom apart over an argument about ambition. Virk was the result: a fully sovereign nation carved cleanly out of Firk's old territory, on Firk's border, but answering to no one but itself.

Ethnically and culturally, Virk is barely distinguishable from Firk — the same **human and gnome** majority, the same **dwarf and halfling** minorities — which makes its insistence on being utterly its own nation something of a running joke among its neighbors. Virk doesn't care. It has its own council, its own small but sharply drilled militia, and a genuine, if slightly desperate, determination to matter on a map crowded with older, larger names.

### Growing Pains

Virk's youth shows most in its military and diplomacy alike: enthusiastic, underfunded, and prone to overcommitting to alliances it can't fully back up. Its army is small, well-trained by necessity rather than tradition, and disproportionately officered by the same restless nobles who pushed for independence in the first place — a nation still run largely by the generation that founded it, for better and occasionally for worse.

Firk, for its part, watches its offshoot with a complicated mix of pride and exasperation — the diplomatic equivalent of a parent who let their kid move out ten years ago and is trying very hard not to comment on how the apartment's being run. The two nations remain close, trade heavily, and would almost certainly come to each other's aid in a real crisis. Whether Virk would ever admit it needed that help is, characteristically, a separate question entirely.
