Illipand is old in the way that makes younger nations uneasy — not frail with age, but settled, the way a stone is settled after a thousand years of tide. Its capital sits where the dunes finally give way to deep-water harbors, and for as long as anyone can trace, Illipand's ports have moved more gold than its armies ever moved soldiers. The old families who run its trade houses don't call themselves nobility, though everyone treats them as such; their power was never granted by a crown, only compounded, generation after generation, until refusing their terms became more expensive than accepting them.

What makes Illipand dangerous isn't its navy, though it has one — it's the fact that half the continent's wars have been quietly bankrolled from its counting houses, usually on both sides at once. Illipand's old magic isn't the flashy sort taught in academies; it's woven into contracts, wards on vaults, and bloodlines that have kept their gifts within the same handful of merchant-mage families for centuries. Outsiders who marry into Illipand's old houses often find the magic doesn't take to them the way it takes to those born there — a detail the families have never bothered to explain, and never needed to.

Illipand's relationship with its neighbors is transactional above all else. It has no particular loyalty to East or West Masterg, Yonia, or even Chatillinquis's rising ambitions — only an interest in whichever outcome keeps its harbors open and its ledgers favorable. Some in Illipand quietly profit from Chatillinquis's expansion, selling grain, steel, and information to an empire they'd never dream of inviting onto their own soil. Whether that arrangement survives once Chatillinquis stops needing Illipand's gold is a question the trade houses have started asking each other in rooms with no windows.

Socially, Illipand prizes composure above almost everything — raised voices, visible anger, or open desperation are treated as a kind of failure, regardless of the reason behind them. Its beaches and dune-side villas have made it a place wealthy exiles from across Malagaerûn quietly retire to, provided they can afford the city's prices and its silence.
